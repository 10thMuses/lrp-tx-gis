## Post 1 — Fuel cells (item 2) · pair with li-fuelcells-2026-06-18.png

The most overlooked trade in AI power is a fuel cell.

The market has framed the AI power crunch as a two-horse race: gas now, nuclear later. Both horses are stuck at the gate.

Gas is the bridge — but the bridge is sold out. GE Vernova has roughly 10 GW of turbine capacity left across 2029 and 2030 combined, with new bids pricing 10–20 points higher on $/kW; Siemens is booked through 2028. Nuclear is the destination — but SMRs and restarts don't deliver until 2030+.

That leaves a gap across 2026–2029 that nothing in the headline debate can fill. The overlooked answer is already being built and financed: on-site fuel cells.

Why they're underpriced:
• No queue. No interconnection wait, no turbine slot — modular units deploy in roughly 12–18 months, behind the fence.
• The capital has already moved. Bloom and Oracle signed a master agreement for up to 2.8 GW of solid-oxide fuel cells, with 1.2 GW underway. Brookfield committed up to $5B to finance Bloom deployments — institutional money underwriting fuel cells as an asset class, not a science project.
• Policy is clearing the lane. Senator Cotton's DATA Act would create an off-grid "consumer-regulated electric utility" category for behind-the-fence load — exactly as FERC (PJM co-location) and Texas SB6 raise the cost of leaning on the grid.

The mispricing: the bridge-power equity narrative still revolves around gas turbines and reactors, while the only generation that actually ships into the 2026–2029 window gets treated as a niche. The reprice catalyst won't be a groundbreaking — it'll be the second and third hyperscaler fuel-cell master agreement, and the first fuel-cell ABS or project-finance print. That's when the market stops calling it a pilot and starts pricing it as infrastructure.

The overlooked asset is the one with no waiting line.

## Post 2 — Residual-value guarantee (item 3) · pair with li-rvg-2026-06-18.png

$29.5B financed. The real risk is one footnote.

Everyone reported the headline on Meta and Blue Owl's Hyperion data center: $27B of debt plus $2.5B of equity, an A+ rating at +225 bp over Treasuries, anchored by PIMCO (~$18B) and BlackRock (~$3B). S&P and Morgan Stanley are already calling it a "template transaction."

The size is noise. The signal is how an A+ rating attached to a vehicle with a thin ~8–10% equity cushion. It required one thing: a 16-year Meta residual-value guarantee absorbing the tech-obsolescence risk.

Hold that next to the asset. The GPUs inside depreciate over 3 to 5 years. The guarantee runs 16. The hyperscaler moved the asset off its balance sheet but kept the economic risk through a long-dated contingent guarantee — and the rating agencies blessed it.

That is synthetic leverage. And it isn't one deal. As the field copies the "template," the sector accumulates a stack of off-balance-sheet, long-dated obsolescence guarantees that no consolidated balance sheet shows. If GPU useful life compresses — and every product cycle argues it might — those guarantees are the contingent liability that bites across the whole sector at once.

The contrarian read: stop reading the deal size and start reading the guarantee structure. The tell to watch in the next hyperscaler SPV is whether the residual-value guarantee tenor keeps stretching while the equity cushion keeps thinning. That is the sector quietly levering up in exactly the place credit analysts aren't looking.

The number on the press release is not the risk. The duration mismatch in the footnote is.
