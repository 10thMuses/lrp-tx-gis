## Post 1 — Fuel cells (item 2) · pair with li-fuelcells-2026-06-18.png

Gas is the bridge to nuclear. The bridge is sold out until 2029.

The consensus has the shape right: nuclear is the long-term baseload answer for AI, and gas is the bridge that carries demand until the reactors arrive. The part nobody is pricing is that the bridge itself is supply-constrained.

You can sign a gas-fired PPA today and not get the machine for years:
• GE Vernova has roughly 10 GW of turbine capacity left across 2029 and 2030 combined, with new bids pricing 10–20 points higher on $/kW.
• Siemens Energy is booked through 2028.
• Lead times are running at three years and stretching.

So if gas is the bridge and the bridge is sold out, what carries demand until the turbines arrive? On-site generation that ships now — and the capital just voted:
• Bloom and Oracle signed a master agreement for up to 2.8 GW of solid-oxide fuel cells, with 1.2 GW already underway.
• Brookfield committed up to $5B to finance Bloom deployments — turning on-site fuel cells from a science project into a financeable asset class.

Policy is bending the same way. Senator Cotton's DATA Act would create an off-grid "consumer-regulated electric utility" category — a clean statutory lane for behind-the-fence load — exactly as FERC (PJM co-location) and Texas SB6 raise the cost of leaning on the grid.

The contrarian read: fuel cells aren't replacing gas as the bridge, and they aren't replacing nuclear as the destination. They fill the front of the bridge — the 2026–2029 window where the gas turbines simply can't be delivered. For those 36 months the deployable answer is fuel cells and reciprocating engines, not patience. The reprice signal won't be a groundbreaking — it'll be the second and third hyperscaler fuel-cell master agreement, and the first fuel-cell ABS or project-finance print. That's when the market stops calling it a pilot and starts pricing it as infrastructure.

Watch the deals, not the ribbon-cuttings.

## Post 2 — Residual-value guarantee (item 3) · pair with li-rvg-2026-06-18.png

$29.5B financed. The real risk is one footnote.

Everyone reported the headline on Meta and Blue Owl's Hyperion data center: $27B of debt plus $2.5B of equity, an A+ rating at +225 bp over Treasuries, anchored by PIMCO (~$18B) and BlackRock (~$3B). S&P and Morgan Stanley are already calling it a "template transaction."

The size is noise. The signal is how an A+ rating attached to a vehicle with a thin ~8–10% equity cushion. It required one thing: a 16-year Meta residual-value guarantee absorbing the tech-obsolescence risk.

Hold that next to the asset. The GPUs inside depreciate over 3 to 5 years. The guarantee runs 16. The hyperscaler moved the asset off its balance sheet but kept the economic risk through a long-dated contingent guarantee — and the rating agencies blessed it.

That is synthetic leverage. And it isn't one deal. As the field copies the "template," the sector accumulates a stack of off-balance-sheet, long-dated obsolescence guarantees that no consolidated balance sheet shows. If GPU useful life compresses — and every product cycle argues it might — those guarantees are the contingent liability that bites across the whole sector at once.

The contrarian read: stop reading the deal size and start reading the guarantee structure. The tell to watch in the next hyperscaler SPV is whether the residual-value guarantee tenor keeps stretching while the equity cushion keeps thinning. That is the sector quietly levering up in exactly the place credit analysts aren't looking.

The number on the press release is not the risk. The duration mismatch in the footnote is.
